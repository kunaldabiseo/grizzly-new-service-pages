The short answer

How to insulate a food processing facility

Food safety comes first and energy second. Map the building by temperature and moisture, keep every insulated surface in a food area covered and cleanable, stop the cold surfaces that drip condensate over product, and do the work inside your sanitation schedule and food safety program.

  1. 01

    Map the zones

    Hot and wet, ambient, cold and frozen rooms each need a different approach.

  2. 02

    Cover and clean

    No exposed, fibrous or porous insulation where food is handled.

  3. 03

    Stop the drip

    Warm the cold surfaces over lines so condensate never forms above product.

  4. 04

    Follow your program

    GMP rules, foreign material control and a sanitation sign-off before production.

What the food code says

FDA's food plant rules say floors, walls and ceilings must be able to be "adequately cleaned and kept clean and kept in good repair", and that "drip or condensate from fixtures, ducts and pipes does not contaminate food."

21 CFR 117.20(b)(4). Meat and poultry plants under USDA also need walls, floors and ceilings of durable materials impervious to moisture.

Temperature zones

Food processing insulation from cook room to freezer

A food plant runs hot, ambient, cold and frozen rooms under one roof. Most insulation trouble starts where two of those rooms meet. Pick a room to see what goes wrong there and how we handle it.

HotFrozen

Cook and fry

Hot and steamy. Kettles, ovens and fryers put heat and moisture into the air.

What goes wrong

Steam rises and hits a roof deck or ceiling that is colder than the air. Condensate forms and can drip onto product, which is exactly what the food code says must not happen.

How we insulate it

Insulate and seal the roof deck or ceiling above so its inside surface stays warmer than the steamy air, then cover it with a cleanable finish. Exhaust hoods and makeup air have to keep up too, which is your mechanical contractor's side.

Sanitation interface

Work happens with lines covered or removed, outside production, and the zone is cleaned to your pre-op standard before handback.

Drip check

Condensate forms when a surface is colder than the dew point of the air around it. Steamy cook rooms have a high dew point, and a roof deck in winter can be far colder. Move the sliders to see where your room sits.

Coolers and freezers

Ice above the freezer and sweat around the cooler

Coolers and freezers are usually insulated panel boxes built by refrigeration specialists. The trouble often shows up around them: in the space above the box ceiling, at pipe and conduit holes through the panels, and on the warm side of the walls.

Illustrative section
1

Space above the freezer ceiling

Warm, moist building air leaks into the gap between the box ceiling and the roof. It meets the cold panel and turns to frost and ice. We air-seal that space and its openings and insulate the roof above so less moisture gets there.

Whose jobUs for sealing and insulation. Your refrigeration contractor for the box, doors and any heat tracing.

Your food safety program

How a food processing insulation contractor should work in your plant

A crew in a food plant follows the plant's rules, not its own. These are the things we settle with your food safety, sanitation and maintenance leads before work starts.

GMP and entry rules

Your visitor and contractor rules: hairnets, smocks, hand washing, traffic patterns between raw and ready-to-eat areas.

Foreign material control

No loose fibers, no glass, tool counts and covered lines. Debris is bagged at the work zone, not carried through production.

Sanitation windows

Work fits between the last run and the sanitation crew, or into planned shutdowns. Each zone gets a cleanup sign-off.

Pre-op handback

We walk the zone with your team before production restarts, so pre-op inspection is not a surprise.

  • Master sanitation schedule and shutdown dates

Questions

Food processing insulation contractor FAQs

What does a food processing insulation contractor do?

A food processing insulation contractor insulates and air-seals the roof, walls and ceilings of food and beverage plants with finishes that can be cleaned, and plans the work around sanitation schedules and the plant's food safety program. At Grizzly that includes removing old or shedding insulation, sealing around coolers and freezers, and installing cleanable, covered systems.

Can exposed insulation be used in a food processing area?

Usually not. FDA rules require floors, walls and ceilings that can be adequately cleaned and kept clean, and USDA-inspected meat and poultry plants need surfaces of durable materials impervious to moisture. Exposed fibrous or porous insulation does not meet that in food areas, so it goes behind washable liner panels, inside insulated metal panels or under an approved finish your food safety lead accepts.

How do you stop condensation dripping from the ceiling over product?

By keeping the ceiling or roof deck surface warmer than the dew point of the room air. Insulating and sealing the deck does that, and enough exhaust and makeup air keeps steam down. FDA rules say drip or condensate from fixtures, ducts and pipes must not contaminate food.

Why is there ice above our freezer?

Usually warm, moist building air is leaking into the space above the freezer ceiling or through pipe and conduit holes in the panels, then freezing. We air-seal that space and the penetrations and insulate the roof above. The freezer box itself stays with your refrigeration contractor.

Do you build coolers and freezers?

No. Walk-in and warehouse-scale coolers and freezers are built by refrigeration and panel specialists. We handle the building around them: roof, walls, the space above the box and sealing around penetrations.

Can you work without stopping production?

Usually, yes. Work fits between the last run and sanitation, on weekends or in planned shutdowns, one zone at a time. Each zone is cleaned and walked with your team before pre-op inspection.

Does Building Performance Colorado apply to food processing plants?

Often not. Colorado's law leaves out buildings where more than half of the gross floor area is used for manufacturing, industrial or agricultural purposes. A building of 50,000 sq ft or more that is mostly warehouse or cold storage can still be covered, so check how your floor area splits.

Can you send product data sheets for our food safety team to approve?

Yes. We send data sheets and safety data sheets for every material in the scope, so your food safety team can review and approve them before anything comes on site.

Which insulation system is best for a food plant ceiling?

It depends on the room. Wash-down rooms usually get insulated metal panels or insulation enclosed behind washable liner panels. Dry rooms can sometimes take spray foam with a coating your food safety lead accepts. Offices and non-food rooms can use ordinary insulation behind drywall or ceiling tile.

How much does food processing insulation cost?

Food processing insulation cost depends on the finish, the work windows between sanitation shifts, protection and cleanup, any removal, the area and access, and work around cold rooms. We quote from a free walk-through with an itemized scope letter.
